About

I'm a biomedical engineering student captivated by the idea that we can print living tissue — not as science fiction, but as a solvable engineering problem.

My work sits at the crossroads of biology, control systems, and hardware design, where I get to ask questions that don't have clean answers yet. What draws me to this field is the full-stack nature of it: understanding cell biology deeply enough to design around it, then building the physical systems that make precision possible.

  • — Bioprinting & tissue engineering
  • — Control systems for biological hardware
  • — Accessible, low-cost lab instrumentation

02

Research

I approach bioprinting as a control engineering problem — one where the biology sets the constraints and the engineering provides the levers. My interests center on making bioprinting more precise, more accessible, and more biologically faithful.

Primary Focus

Control Engineering
for Bioprinting

Extrusion-based bioprinting is only as good as its consistency. Small variations in flow rate, nozzle pressure, or temperature can compromise cell viability and structural fidelity. My focus is applying closed-loop feedback control — PID controllers, real-time sensor integration, adaptive parameter tuning — to regulate extrusion dynamics and produce repeatable, cell-friendly output.

  • Closed-loop control
  • PID
  • Extrusion dynamics
  • Sensor integration

Secondary Interest

Cornea
Bioprinting

The cornea's highly organized, anisotropic collagen fiber structure and requirement for optical transparency make it one of the most technically demanding targets in tissue engineering. I'm drawn to recreating stromal architecture with sufficient fidelity for light transmission — and by the clinical urgency of global corneal transplant shortages.

  • Corneal stroma
  • Collagen alignment
  • Keratocytes
  • Tissue engineering
Currently exploring — Low-shear bioink rheology and real-time extrusion monitoring approaches.

Upcoming — Summer 2026 P.01

Low-Cost Low-Shear
Extrusion System

Extrusion-based bioprinters subject cells to significant mechanical shear as bioink is forced through narrow nozzles — stress that compromises membrane integrity and reduces post-print viability. Commercial solutions that address this are expensive and inaccessible to most research labs. This project aims to design and build a low-cost extrusion system that minimizes shear stress through careful nozzle geometry and pressure control, making cell-friendly bioprinting accessible without a large equipment budget.

  • Bioprinting
  • Hardware design
  • Extrusion mechanics
  • Shear stress
  • Cell viability
